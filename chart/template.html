CBAM Downstream Map

Extending CBAM downstream: carbon-leakage risk and import dependence, product by product

By Elise Viadere, Centre for European Reform Data and code on GitHub

The EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) aims to levy the same carbon price on imports as EU heavy industry pays.

This graph provides policy-makers with a snapshot of how the CBAM would raise import costs for different products. Those costs are then measured against how dependent the EU market is on imports for that product.

Each circle represents a product which the CBAM might cover. The European Commission, the Council and the European Parliament have proposed differing lists of products. Products in the initial Commission proposal are blue, ones added by the Council are orange, and ones added by the Parliament are green.

The higher the circle, the more the carbon charge adds to the price of the imported product. The further right the circle, the larger the share of the EU market which is supplied by imports from outside the EU. Hover over or tap a circle to see the product.

How to read it: a product is a dependence risk if imports supply more than a third of the EU market (right of the dashed line) and one country supplies more than half of those imports (filled circle). It is a leakage risk if the CBAM charge is at least 1% of the price (on or above the dashed line). The remaining products, including hollow circles at the bottom right, have a low estimated carbon cost and no identified concentrated import dependence.
Why 1% for leakage risk: carbon leakage, a carbon price pushing production abroad, only matters if the charge changes what a product costs. From 2013 to 2020 the Commission's ETS test counted a trade-exposed sector at risk when its carbon costs reached 5% of value added. In EU manufacturing, value added is about a quarter of turnover, so that bar is about 1.25% of price. The 1% line is slightly lower, giving the longer lists the benefit of the doubt. Each list carries less leakage risk than the one before at any line between 1% and 2.5%.
Why a third for dependence risk: like the Commission's review of strategic dependencies, the test asks how much Europe relies on imports and how concentrated they are. But it compares imports with the whole EU market, including what Europe makes itself: a good made mostly at home is not one Europe depends on others for. A third is a demanding bar, since imports from outside the EU meet about 13% of EU demand on average.
What the tests do not show: a significant carbon cost does not mean a factory will relocate, and reliance on one supplier does not mean supplies are insecure. The tests show which products have a stronger case for inclusion in CBAM, not which will leak or be cut off.
Official counts and ours: the Commission proposed about 180 downstream products; we find 178 eight-digit tariff lines. The Council kept them, dropped three and added 200, about 370 lines in all, so the often-quoted "about 200" describes its additions, not its whole list. Parliament's 457 matches the Commission's lines plus the 276 it lists one by one (we find about 450); its broad headings reach 623 lines in all, which is what this map uses. Without them, Parliament's list covers €188bn of imports instead of €238bn. The buttons count the lines each position adds, and colour credits each line to the first position that lists it.
Parliament's position adds broad headings, such as all of chapter 73, to the codes it lists one by one. This analysis takes the widest reading (623 tariff lines, €238 billion of imports a year). Counting only the listed codes gives 444 lines and €188 billion.
CBAM charge: carbon in the steel or aluminium content at €82.32 a tonne of CO₂, the Commission's CBAM certificate price for the third quarter of 2026, once fully phased in. EU market: EU production plus imports from outside the EU.
